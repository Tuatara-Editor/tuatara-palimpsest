#+language: en

* Strengths and how to read a finding

What STRONG, worth a look and for the record mean, and how to weigh them.

Every finding has one of three strengths. The strength says how far an honest
program could have left the same sign. It does not say how much was changed or
how wrong it is. This chapter says what each strength means, how the strength is
chosen, and how to read a finding with its honest causes before you decide.

** The three strengths

#+caption: Table 6.1. The strengths.
| Strength               | It means                                           |
|------------------------+----------------------------------------------------|
| Strong sign of editing | What an honest program does not leave: a date      |
|                        | before the file was made, a white box over text    |
|                        | added later, a cross-reference section where none  |
|                        | is said to be.                                     |
| Worth a look           | What editing leaves, and some honest programs too: |
|                        | a page changed in a later save, a font in two      |
|                        | subsets, a figure in another font.                 |
| For the record         | What the file holds that an examiner will want to  |
|                        | know: a signature, an embedded file, a later save  |
|                        | kept, a PDF editor named.                          |

In the report they are the headings =** Strong signs of editing=,
=** Worth a look= and =** For the record=.

The first is the one to read first. A strong sign is a fact of the file that an
honest program does not produce, not a judgment about the document. A strong
sign can still have an honest meaning in your case: a stamp laid over a page in
a later save is how a viewer signs a contract, and chapter 8 shows that. Say
what was found, and say why it is or is not what you expected.

** How the strength is chosen

The strength belongs to the rule that made the finding, sometimes raised by what
was found. The same covering shape is a strong sign when it is white and worth a
look in another colour. Invisible text is for the record over a scanned page and
strong when a later save added it. Chapter 7 says, for every check, when its
findings are which.

How far a measure is off never sets the strength. The least amounts a rule names
(30 grey levels of ink, 1.5 pixels, a tenth of a size) decide only whether a
finding is made. A run of text in a size of its own is worth a look whether it
is a tenth smaller or half, as a heading or a footnote is too. What raises a
finding is a sign honest programs do not leave: pixels the same where no scanner
makes them so, or a structure no program writes.

A scanned or photographed word unlike its line is never strong, however many
ways it is unlike it, for a real scan has such words too. When two measures
apart from each other agree on one, so that it looks unlike its line and sits
off it, the finding says so, and it stays worth a look.

A fact of how the file was made is for the record, said once however many
fields or ways show it: a later save kept, a PDF editor named, a picture kept
otherwise than the others, a JPEG saved twice.
Honest files show each as often as edited ones, and none of them points at a
place. What a later save changed is the sign, and that stays worth a look.

A sign is for the record too where the honest files measured show it as often as
the forged places do, and it was found at no forged place alone: a word off its
line by its baseline alone by more than 5 pixels, a block of lines set apart,
near pairs of pieces or on a page naming more than five, the lines a file sets
apart throughout, hidden text of fewer than three letters. Each is noted with
its place, for the examiner to look at when the case asks.

** Where a sign lies

A forger changes what is worth changing: an amount, a total, a date, a name, an
account or reference number, an IBAN, a signature, a form's filled field. An
honest scan's quirks fall anywhere. So where the page's text can be read there
(its own text, or a scan's text recognition), a sign is weighed by the line it
lies on. A line holding a figure or the label of such a field is a place worth
changing, and a sign there stays worth a look. A line of five words or more
without either is running text or boilerplate (terms and conditions, a letter's
body, a printed notice): a sign of a kind honest files show too lies there as a
note, which says that honest pages show it and that a change there would serve
no one. A short line without figures may be a name or a heading, and a scan
without text cannot be read at all; a sign there stays worth a look. A sign no
honest file showed stays worth a look wherever it lies.

A finding worth a look of a kind honest files show says so in a sentence of its
own: how many of the 977 honest files measured show it, and its known honest
causes. Weigh it against them before deciding.

Strong signs need the file to contradict itself: a =startxref= naming a section
outside its save, a length that runs over a save of the file, a date before the
file was made. A file that does not contradict itself gets, at most, a worth a
look.

** The verdict and the order

The verdict in the summary is the strongest strength found. The findings are
listed strongest first, then by revision, then by page. Those worth a look go
in three parts first: signs no honest file measured showed, then signs on a
place worth changing, then the rest. Each is put in the
revision it is about, or, when it is about the file as a whole, in the latest.

** Reading a finding

1. Read the sentence. It says what was found and what it means.
2. Read where: the revision, the page and the objects. ~RET~ on an object
   number shows it as that revision has it. ~C-c p d~ draws the page with the
   object framed.
3. Read the honest causes. A finding that can come from honest programs names
   them, for example heavy type in a scan, a stamp or a clipped scanner. Open
   the place it points to and see whether one of them fits.
4. Open the revision before (chapter 3) and compare it with the last. The
   report sets the text of each changed page side by side.
5. Open the log for the finding to see the rule, the evidence and the steps to
   check it by hand.
6. Weigh what is outside the file: the other party's copy, the paper, how the
   file reached you.

** Checks that could not finish

In the log each finding names its check, and the =* Checks= part of the report
sums the findings up by check.

A check that could not finish says so, for the record. Treat that as a limit,
not as a clean result: what the check would have read was not read.

#+begin_try Try it, reading a finding by its strength
1. Examine a PDF of your own that has a later save, with =M-x palimpsest=.
2. In =* Findings=, read the groups in order. Note which finding sets the
   verdict.
3. For one finding worth a look, read the honest causes in its sentence and open
   the object it names with ~RET~.
4. Open the log of the revision it is in with ~RET~ on its name under =*
   Revisions=, and read the =Rule= and the =Steps to see it by hand=. Press ~q~
   to close the log.
#+end_try
